Module 1 is the gate

Accuracy on the first module decides whether the second is the harder form — and only the harder form reaches the top of the scale.

So never rush Module 1 to bank time. It is not a warm-up.

The order never changes

Domains appear in the same sequence on every test: Craft & Structure → Information & Ideas → Conventions → Expression of Ideas.

Within a domain, easiest to hardest. Conventions is the exception — sorted by difficulty alone, sub-types mixed.

Never leave a blank

No negative marking. An unanswered question is a guaranteed zero; a guess is a free 25%.

Flag, guess, move. Return only if time remains.

What this predictability buys you Knowing exactly where the grammar block sits inside the module means you can budget the clock before the test starts — spend 4 minutes on the seven Conventions questions and hand the surplus to Cross-Text Connections, which is where the section is actually decided.

The realistic arithmetic

Taking Conventions and Expression from roughly two-thirds correct to near-perfect is about 7–8 questions. On the Reading & Writing scale that is worth in the region of 60–80 points, from rules alone.

The same three weeks spent on inference questions would move almost nothing. This is the whole argument for the sequence.

Standard English Conventions · part one

Boundaries: one question, asked every time.

Ignore what sounds right. Look at what sits on each side of the punctuation mark, and label it independent or dependent. Every boundary question collapses once you do.

JoinPunctuationRule
IND · IND. or ;Always legal. Two complete sentences may always be separated by a period or a semicolon.
IND · IND, + FANBOYSComma plus for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. The comma alone is never enough.
IND · anything:The left side must be a complete sentence. The right side explains, lists or specifies it.
DEP · IND,A lead-in clause or phrase takes a comma before the main sentence starts.
IND , IND✗ neverThe comma splice. The single most common wrong answer on the test.

Supplements non-essential information

Extra information is fenced by a matched pair — two commas, two dashes or two brackets. Never mix one with another.

Test: delete everything between the fences. What remains must still be a complete, sensible sentence.

✓ The paper, published in 1972, was ignored.
✓ The paper — published in 1972 — was ignored.
✗ The paper, published in 1972 — was ignored.

Essential vs non-essential

No commas around information that identifies which one you mean. Commas around information that merely adds colour.

✓ The scientist who found it won.
✓ Marie Curie, who found it, won.

In the first, the clause is doing identifying work — remove it and you no longer know which scientist. In the second, the name has already identified her.

Elimination hack If two answer choices are grammatically equivalent — a period and a semicolon between the same two clauses, say — then neither can be correct. A question cannot have two right answers. This alone kills two of four choices on a good number of boundary items.
Standard English Conventions · part two

Six rules cover almost everything else.

1 · Subject–verb agreement

Find the true subject. Mentally delete every prepositional phrase and clause sitting between it and the verb — they are placed there deliberately.

The set of samples was tested.
The results, despite the delay, were clear.

2 · Pronouns

Number must match the antecedent, and the antecedent must be unambiguous.

its = belonging to it
it's = it is
who = subject   whom = object
their / there / they're

3 · Verb tense

The passage sets a timeline; the verb must sit on it. Look outside the underlined portion for the tense already established.

Sequences must stay consistent — a narrative that begins in the past does not drift into the present without a reason on the page.

4 · Modifiers

The noun immediately after an opening descriptive phrase must be the thing being described. This is a rule about word order, not meaning.

✓ Founded in 1887, the museum grew.
✗ Founded in 1887, visitors flocked.

5 · Parallel structure

Items in a list or a comparison share grammatical form — all nouns, all -ing, all infinitives.

✓ reading, writing and editing
✗ reading, writing and to edit

6 · Apostrophes

students — more than one
student's — belonging to one
students' — belonging to several
its' — never exists

Possessive pronouns — its, hers, theirs, yours, ours — never take an apostrophe.

Time discipline A Conventions question should take 30–40 seconds. If one is taking longer, you are reading for meaning instead of testing a rule. Every second saved here funds Cross-Text Connections later in the module.
Expression of Ideas · 8–12 Q

Two question types. Both mechanical, both routinely fumbled.

Transitions ~5–8 Q

Cover the answer choices. Read the sentence before and the sentence after. Name the relationship in your own word — then, and only then, look for the choice that matches it.

Reading the options first is exactly how a plausible-sounding wrong answer talks you into it. Every wrong choice is a real transition word used correctly for a relationship that isn't there.

add   furthermore · moreover · in addition
contrast   however · nevertheless · by contrast · still
cause   therefore · consequently · thus · as a result
example   for instance · specifically · namely
sequence   subsequently · meanwhile · then
concede   admittedly · granted · to be sure

Rhetorical Synthesis ~3–5 Q

Read the goal in the question stem first — before the bulleted notes. The goal is the entire question. The notes are only raw material.

Typical goals: emphasise a similarity, introduce X to an unfamiliar reader, present the second study's finding.

  • The right answer does exactly the stated goal — no moreDoing the goal plus something extra is a wrong answer
  • It uses only facts present in the notesAnything added from outside is wrong however true
  • Wrong answers are accurate, fluent, and answer a different goalThis is why the stem must be read first — otherwise every choice looks fine
Why this domain is worth the most per hour Neither question type requires understanding the subject matter. A transition question about mycorrhizal networks and one about Ming ceramics are the same question. Once the procedure is automatic, the content becomes irrelevant — which is not true anywhere else in the section.
Craft and Structure · ~28%

Predict first. Always. Without exception.

Every question type in this domain has the same defence: form your own answer before reading theirs. The wrong choices are engineered to be attractive to a reader who arrives without one.

Words in Context ~6–8 Q

Cover the choices. Read the sentence with a blank and write your own word. Then find the closest match.

  • Traps are the secondary meanings of familiar words"qualify" as in limit, "novel" as in new, "sound" as in valid
  • If two choices are near-synonyms, both are wrongThere can only be one answer
  • The sentence always contains the clue — a contrast word, a restatement, an example

Text Structure & Purpose ~3–4 Q

Ask what the sentence does, not what it says. Answer in a verb before you look.

introduces · illustrates · qualifies
counters · concedes · concludes
defines · contextualises · complicates

A choice that accurately summarises the content but names the wrong function is the standard wrong answer here.

Cross-Text Connections ~2 Q

The hardest questions in the section, and worth the extra time.

Before looking at anything, write one line: what would Author 2 say about Author 1's specific claim — agree, disagree, extend, or qualify? Then find that line among the choices.

Budget 100+ seconds each. This is what the Conventions surplus is for.

Information and Ideas · ~26%

The answer must be forced by the text, not merely allowed by it.

Central Ideas and Details ~3–4 Q

The answer must cover the whole passage, not one true sentence inside it. A choice that is accurate about the second half only is the classic trap.

Inferences ~3–4 Q

Usually phrased "which most logically completes the text". Read the logical connective in the final sentence — therefore, however, because — it tells you the shape of what must follow.

If you have to add an assumption to make a choice work, it is wrong. Consistent with the text is not the same as demanded by it.

Command of Evidence — Textual ~4–5 Q

Name the exact claim to be supported, in your own words, first. Then test each quotation with one question: does this make the claim more likely to be true?

Not "is this on the same topic". Relevance is the trap; support is the test.

Command of Evidence — Quantitative ~2–3 Q

  • Do not read the whole graph. Read the claim, then find the single data point that decides it
  • Check axis labels and units before anything elseThousands vs millions, percentage vs count — this is where the question is won or lost
  • Wrong answers describe the data correctly but do not support the stated claim
Expectation setting This is the domain that improves slowest, and three weeks will not transform it. The realistic goal here is not losing points to method — misreading what is being asked, choosing a true statement over a responsive one. The underlying comprehension gains come later, if there is a later.
Traps & timing

Every wrong answer is one of four things.

1 · Half right

The first clause is correct and the second is false. Students choose it because they stop reading once the opening matches. Read to the end of every choice.

2 · True but not asked

An accurate statement about the passage that does not answer the question. The defence is knowing precisely what was asked before you look at the options.

3 · Too extreme

always · never · proves · all · must · impossible — the SAT hedges. Right answers say suggests, may, tends to.

4 · Out of scope

Plausible from general knowledge, absent from the text. If you cannot point to the line that supports it, it is not the answer.

Three weeks

The protocol to 12 September.

Roughly 2½ hours a day on this section, alongside the Math track. Bluebook, the official Question Bank and Khan Academy — nothing else.

  1. 20–21 Aug · BaselineBluebook Practice Test 4, full length, timed, cold. Do not study first — a baseline you have prepared for tells you nothing.
  2. 22–28 Aug · Conventions and Expression onlyAll six grammar rules and the boundary table to automatic. 40 questions a day from the Question Bank, filtered to these two domains. Error log from the first question.
  3. 29 Aug · Practice Test 6Full length, same start time as the real test. Check whether the rule work has landed before moving on.
  4. 30 Aug – 4 Sep · Craft & Structure, Information & IdeasPredict-before-you-look on every single question. Khan Academy targeted practice generated from the Bluebook results.
  5. 5 Sep · Practice Test 8
  6. 6–8 Sep · No new materialRe-attempt every logged error from all three tests. This is the highest-value block in the three weeks and the one most likely to be skipped.
  7. 9 Sep · Practice Test 11 — finalThree days out, and the test that most closely reflects current administrations — so the last signal before the sitting is also the most predictive one. Late enough to be diagnostic, early enough to act on.
  8. 10–11 Sep · TaperBluebook exam setup completed, admission ticket printed, passport in the bag. Friday: nothing at all.
