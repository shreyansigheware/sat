Desmos on every question

A full graphing calculator is built into Bluebook and permitted throughout. Most students never learn to use it properly — that is the largest single lever in this section.

Reference sheet always on screen

Areas, volumes, Pythagoras, special right triangles, degrees and radians. Nothing on it needs memorising. Everything not on it does.

Module 1 is the gate

Accuracy on the first module decides whether the second is the harder form, and only the harder form reaches 800. Never sacrifice Module 1 accuracy for speed.

The counter-intuitive risk

Strong mathematicians lose more points to speed than to difficulty. Confidence in the content produces skimmed question stems — answering for x when the question asked for 2x, or for the cost per unit when it asked for the total.

The audit on slide 8 exists specifically for this, and it is the single most important habit in the Math track.

Tooling

Six Desmos moves that turn algebra into looking at a picture.

These take about two days to make reflexive and they pay for themselves on every practice test afterwards. Learn them in week one, before anything else.

1 · Solve anything

Type the equation exactly as printed. Desmos plots both sides and marks the intersection — click it to read the solution.

3x + 7 = x² − 5
→ click the grey intersection dots

Works for quadratics, exponentials, absolute value and radicals identically.

2 · Systems

Type both equations on separate lines; the intersection is the solution. Faster and far safer than elimination under time pressure.

It also answers how many solutions at a glance — no intersection, one, or a line lying on top of a line.

3 · Roots, vertex, intercepts

Type the function; Desmos labels zeros, minima, maxima and both intercepts as clickable points with exact coordinates.

Every "what is the minimum value of the function" question becomes a click.

4 · Sliders for unknown constants

The hardest recurring set-up: "for what value of k does the system have exactly one solution?"

y = x² + kx + 9
y = 3
→ add slider for k, drag until tangent

5 · Tables and regression

Enter a data table, then fit a line with y1 ~ mx1 + b. Gives the line of best fit, its slope, and predicted values.

Turns most two-variable data questions into a lookup — and it is the doorway to the regression method on the next slide, which is a far bigger lever than best-fit lines.

6 · Inequalities

Type with < or > and Desmos shades the region. "Which point satisfies both inequalities" becomes visual.

When not to reach for it Questions that ask for an answer in terms of x, symbolic simplification, and anything where typing takes longer than solving. Desmos is for finding numbers, not for manipulating symbols. One important exception: "can be rewritten as" and "is equivalent to", when constants are involved, are regression questions — see the next two slides.
Tooling · the move that finishes the section early

Regression is a solver, not a statistics tool.

The six moves opposite handle questions that have a picture. This one handles the questions that don't: anything of the form find the constants that make this true. It is the single least-known high-scorer technique on the Math section, and it has real guardrails — used carelessly it returns confident nonsense.

1 · The one keystroke

Replace = with ~. Desmos stops trying to graph the line and starts solving for every letter that isn't x or y, reporting each in the panel underneath.

= graphs it  →  needs the constants known
~ fits it  →  hands you the constants

2 · Unknown constants in an identity

The recurring set-up: an equation that must hold for every value of x, asking for a, b or their sum. Define a list, retype the equation in x₁, and read the answer off.

x₁ = [2, 3, 5, 7, 11]
a·x₁ + b ~ 4x₁ − 9
→ panel reports a and b

3 · Choose the list properly

The values must be distinct, and must avoid 0 and 1. Those two satisfy far too many relationships by accident, and the constants that come back will fit nothing else.

Small primes are the safe habit — nothing about them is accidentally true. x₁ = random(10) is the faster default and is safer still, since it cannot collide with the question's own numbers.

4 · Enough values for the unknowns

At least as many list values as constants being solved for. Fewer, and the fit is underdetermined and silently wrong.

One exception worth memorising: standard form ax + by = c will not resolve this way however many points you supply. Rearrange to y = first.

5 · Custom forms

The built-in exponential fit assumes one shape. A printed form such as a·b^(x−c) has to be typed out as its own regression.

Skip that and the panel returns awkward decimals that look like an answer and are not one. If the printed form isn't the fitted form, the numbers are meaningless.

6 · Verify before trusting

Regression returns numbers even when no solution exists. It fits the closest thing it can find and reports it without complaint.

Substitute the constants back and confirm both sides coincide. Note also that no solutions and infinitely many both display as an empty graph — that pair has to be told apart deliberately, never by eye.

Three things that silently break Desmos Only lowercase x graphs. A question phrased in a or t plots nothing at all — substitute, or attach a slider.  Angle mode is a trap: check degrees against radians before any trigonometry, because the wrong mode returns a clean, plausible, wrong number.  A list needs brackets when it sits outside a function, or Desmos cannot interpret it.
Tooling · where the last 40 points live

Six set-ups that look unsolvable and take thirty seconds.

These are the recurring hard-module patterns. Each one is a fixed procedure, and each is worth rehearsing until the set-up is written before the question is fully read.

1 · A function plus given points

P(t) = c·d^t, told P(2)=48 and P(5)=1296, asked for P(6). Define the function on its own line first, then register the points as a list on a separate line.

P(t) = c·d^t
[P(2), P(5)] = [48, 1296]
→ then just type P(6)

2 · Percent chains

Three or four linked percentage statements ending in "what is P?". Type each sentence in as its own line, literally, in the order printed, and read P off.

A letter may be reused across lines as long as each statement relates it to different other letters. Doing this by hand is where the errors come from.

3 · Fulfil the stated conditions

When the question says k > m > 47, or "p is an integer greater than 1", or "n and k are positive", pick concrete values that satisfy it before reading anything off.

Ignoring the condition is the single most common way these questions are lost — the panel still returns a number, and the number is wrong.

4 · Integer conditions

"...where j and k are positive integers" and the fit returns decimals. Step j through 1, 2, 3… until both come out whole.

Usually exactly one value works, which is what makes the answer unique.

5 · The dummy factor

Told an expression has a factor of kx + j. A factor alone is not equivalent to the expression — multiply it by an invented second factor of matching shape, then fit.

(kx₁ + j)(dx₁ + z) ~ [the expression]
→ degrees and terms now match

6 · Sliders to maximise

"What is the greatest possible value of ac?" Fit as usual, add a slider to one constant, widen its bounds, and drag while watching the target value rise and turn over.

The peak is the answer. Sliders are for optimising — for plain solving, a defined x₁ is faster and less fiddly.

The two errors that ruin regressions Never write f(x₁). It looks reasonable and does nothing useful — subscripting the argument of a defined function solves no question on this test.  Never put the function's own definition inside the regression line. Define the function first, fit on a separate line. Both mistakes return output rather than an error, which is exactly why they cost points.
Technique

When the algebra is ugly, use the choices.

Back-solve numbers in the choices

When the four choices are plain numbers and the question asks for a value, test one instead of solving.

  • Start with B or C. Choices are printed in ascending order, so one test usually tells you which direction to goOften resolves in two attempts rather than four
  • Best on: awkward word-problem set-ups, "which value satisfies", systems with unpleasant coefficients
  • It converts an algebra problem into arithmetic, which is far harder to get wrong under time

Pick numbers variables in the choices

When the answer choices contain letters, substitute your own value.

  • Choose small and safe — avoid 0 and 1, and avoid any number already in the problemThey make different choices agree by accident
  • Compute the target value, then test all four choicesIf two survive, run a second number — do not guess between them
  • For percentage questions, start from 100
The discipline Both techniques cost time. Use them when the direct route isn't visible within about fifteen seconds — not as a default. A strong algebra student who back-solves everything will run out of clock in Module 2.
Algebra + Advanced Math · 26–30 Q

The six things that actually recur.

Not a syllabus — a list of the specific configurations the SAT builds questions from, year after year. Recognising the set-up is most of the work.

Solution counts of a linear system

m₁ = m₂, b₁ ≠ b₂ → no solution
m₁ = m₂, b₁ = b₂ → infinite
m₁ ≠ m₂ → exactly one

In ax + by = c form, compare ratios: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ means no solution.

The discriminant

b² − 4ac
> 0 → two real solutions
= 0 → exactly one
< 0 → none

This is how nearly every "exactly one solution" question is constructed. Recognise it and the question is one line long.

Three forms of a quadratic

ax² + bx + c → y-intercept
a(x − p)(x − q) → roots
a(x − h)² + k → vertex

The question tells you which form you want. Vertex also at x = −b/2a.

Exponential models

growth y = a(1 + r)ᵗ
decay y = a(1 − r)ᵗ

a is the initial value. The trap is the time unit — a rate given per year with t measured in months, or halving expressed as a percentage.

Function notation & transformations

f(x) + k → up k
f(x + k) → left k
−f(x) → flip over x-axis
f(−x) → flip over y-axis

f(a) = b simply means the graph passes through (a, b).

Radicals & rationals

  • Always check for extraneous solutions after squaring both sidesThe SAT includes the extraneous root among the choices
  • A rational equation excludes any value making a denominator zero
  • Pure simplification is done by hand — factor, expand, difference of squaresBut "rewritten in the form … where a and b are constants" is a regression question, not a hand one
Data & statistics · ~15%

The gap here is vocabulary, not arithmetic.

Every question in this domain is arithmetically trivial. They are missed because a statistical term means something specific that the everyday word does not.

Percentages

percent change = (new − old) / old
  • "Percent of" ≠ "percent more than"120% of 50 is 60; 120% more than 50 is 110
  • A 20% rise followed by a 20% fall does not return to the start

Rates and units

Write the unit fraction and cancel explicitly. Most errors in this domain are unit errors, not calculation errors — minutes against hours, cm against m, per item against per batch.

Underline the unit the question asks for before you begin.

Centre and spread

  • Mean moves with outliers; median does notAdding one very large value: mean rises, median barely shifts
  • Standard deviation = clustering. Comparing two sets, the more spread out one has the larger SD
  • Range and standard deviation answer different questions

Two-variable data

Read predictions off the line of best fit, not off the nearest data point. A common question compares what the model predicts against what was actually observed.

Probability

Almost always read from a two-way table. The denominator is whatever the question restricts you to — "given that the student is in Year 2" means the Year 2 total, not the grand total.

Inference & study design

  • A larger sample narrows the margin of error
  • Conclusions extend only to the population actually sampled
  • Random selection → you may generalise. Random assignment → you may claim cause. Only both permits a causal claim about a wider group
Geometry & trigonometry · ~15%

Know what the reference sheet doesn't give you.

Provided on screen — don't memorise

  • Circle area and circumference; rectangle and triangle area
  • Pythagoras
  • Special right triangles30-60-90 → x, x√3, 2x  ·  45-45-90 → x, x, x√2
  • Volumes: prism, cylinder, sphere, cone, pyramid
  • 360° in a circle, 2π radians, 180° in a triangle

Not provided — memorise these

sin(x°) = cos(90° − x°)
circle: (x−h)² + (y−k)² = r²
arc length = (θ/360) · 2πr

Similar triangles

The most-used tool that the question will never name. Parallel lines and shared angles create similar triangles; set up the ratio of corresponding sides and solve.

Draw it

When no figure is given, draw one and label every value stated before starting. Geometry questions without a diagram are missed at several times the rate of ones with.

Figures are not to scale

Unless explicitly stated. Never measure or estimate from the picture — but do use it to keep track of what you have worked out.

Mechanics · ~11 grid-ins per test

Free points, lost to formatting.

Student-produced response rules

  • 5 characters maximum for a positive answer, 6 including the sign for a negative
  • Fractions accepted; improper fractions are fineMixed numbers are not. Enter 7/2 or 3.5 — never 3 1/2
  • No commas in numbers above 999. No % sign, no $ sign, no units
  • A decimal must fill the available space2/3 is entered as .6666 or .6667 — never .67
  • If more than one value is correct, enter only one of them
✓ 3.5   7/2   −12   .6667   1250
✗ 3 1/2   1,250   45%   $18   .67

The careless-error audit

Content isn't the gap, so the error log tags causes, not topics. Every practice miss gets exactly one tag:

wrong quantity — solved for x, wanted 2x
unit mismatch — minutes vs hours
sign error
misread — not / least / except
transcription — copied a term wrong
grid-in format

Whichever tag repeats becomes a written checking rule you apply before submitting every answer. That rule is the entire Math prep plan.

Timing Target 28 minutes for the 22 questions, leaving 7 minutes to revisit everything flagged. No question takes more than three minutes on the first pass. Then spend the reserve re-reading question stems, not redoing arithmetic — the wrong-quantity error is caught by re-reading, never by recalculating.
